Chapter 5 of 11 — Earth

The Goldilocks Zone

Every star has a "just right" ring around it — not so close that water boils away, not so far that it freezes solid. Scientists call this the Goldilocks zone, like the porridge in the story: not too hot, not too cold.

Just right
Earth landed in exactly the right spot: liquid water covers 71% of its surface, and a blanket of nitrogen and oxygen gas traps just enough warmth. Remember Venus, wrapped in thick clouds and baking at 465°C? That's what happens too close. Remember Mars, thin-aired and frozen at -60°C? That's too far. Earth sits right in between.
Chapter 5 of 11 — Earth

Why We Have Seasons

Earth's axis stays tilted the same way in space all year, like a spinning top leaning to one side. As Earth travels around the Sun, that tilt means different hemispheres catch more direct, warming sunlight at different times.

Spring (Northern Hemisphere)
Right now the Northern and Southern halves get fairly equal light — this in-between point is called an equinox.

Chapter 7 of 11 — Jupiter

Day and Night

A day on Jupiter lasts only about 10 hours — the fastest spin of any planet, even though it's the biggest! Jupiter has no solid ground to stand on, so "day and night" here means which side of its swirling clouds currently faces the Sun — that sunlit side is genuinely warmer than the dark side, even though nobody could ever stand on either one.

Chapter 9 of 11 — Uranus

Rolling on Its Side

Every other planet spins mostly upright, like a top. Uranus doesn't — it's tipped over so far that it basically rolls around the Sun on its side. Drag the slider to compare it to an upright planet.

Tilt: 0°
Scientists think a huge object, maybe close to the size of Earth, crashed into Uranus long ago and knocked it almost completely over. One real result of that extreme tilt: each pole gets about 42 years of nonstop daylight in a row, followed by about 42 years of nonstop darkness!
Chapter 9 of 11 — Uranus

Day and Night

A day on Uranus lasts about 17 hours. Uranus is an icy gas world with no solid ground either — but because it's tipped so far on its side, each pole gets about 42 years of continuous sunlight on its clouds, then 42 years of continuous darkness!

Chapter 10 of 11 — Neptune

Fastest Winds Known

Neptune gives off more heat from deep inside itself than it receives from the distant Sun. Scientists think that extra energy, combined with very little friction to slow things down, helps drive incredibly powerful winds. Tap to measure them.

0 mph
1,200 miles per hour is faster than the strongest hurricanes and tornadoes ever recorded on Earth — and scientists are still studying exactly why Neptune's winds get so extreme.
Chapter 10 of 11 — Neptune

Day and Night

A day on Neptune lasts about 16 hours, even way out at the icy edge of the solar system. Neptune is a gas and ice world with no solid surface, so its day and night are simply the sunlit and shadowed sides of its deep, windy atmosphere.

Pluto: Tiny but Famous

Tap Pluto to uncover the giant heart-shaped glacier that made headlines when a spacecraft finally photographed it up close in 2015.

Tap here: why isn't Pluto a full planet anymore?
In 2006 Pluto was reclassified as a "dwarf planet." A full planet's gravity is strong enough to clear away nearby debris — Pluto shares its neighborhood with lots of other icy objects, so it's grouped with dwarf planets like Eris, Ceres, and Makemake instead.

Asteroids and Comets

Between Mars and Jupiter sits the asteroid belt — millions of rocky leftover chunks from when the solar system formed, all orbiting the Sun together. Tap to scatter some.

Comets are giant "dirty snowballs" of ice, dust, and rock that swing in from the outer solar system. As one gets closer to the Sun, its ice starts to vaporize, streaming out into a glowing tail that always points away from the Sun. Drag the comet closer and watch the tail grow.
